The following are situations in which litigation frequently arises. Fortunately, the SC Owner Disclosure Section V covers many of these issues, again placing the responsibility of disclosure on the owners, not the real estate licensees. However, as previously mentioned, the form does not address all of the following:

Failure to disclose to a buyer that an addition or alteration was performed without a building permit or was not in compliance with code requirements. Courts have held licensees liable for failing to verify that a seller obtained a building permit and final inspection. If necessary, the licensee should check with the local building department.
Failure to disclose previous water leaks. Licensees should advise sellers to disclose water leaks or drainage problems even if they have been corrected. Complaints about concealment can be avoided by disclosure of all leaks, including when the leak occurred, the extent of the resulting damage, and how the damage was repaired.
Disputed property boundaries. Licensees should refrain from making representations with respect to boundary lines. Surveying is beyond the licensed expertise of real estate brokers and sales associates.
Failure to disclose pet urine contamination. The buyer should be advised to retain a qualified expert if such contamination is suspected or is of concern to the buyers.
Failure to investigate and disclose red flags pointing to prior land movement or excessive settling. These may include sloping floors, distorted garage frames, cracks at window or door corners, or cracks in a fireplace. Buyers should be advised to retain a qualified property inspector if any of these or similar red flags are apparent.
Poor inspections. When inspections go wrong, licensees are sometimes sued even when the inspector was selected by the client.
Improper pest control work.
Failure to investigate mold, fungus, or mildew.
