Disclosure requires that owners are aware of the problem; it does not apply to problems of which they are not aware. From a number of lawsuits against the owner, another doctrine emerged—the responsibility of the buyer to "discover" problems about which the buyers are concerned.

Example:

An elderly couple indicated that there was no problem with the plumbing in the hall bathroom. The plumbing was as old as the house (i.e., 48 years). After a family bought the home and started to take many more showers than the couple had taken, it was clear that the plumbing was very outdated and needed to be replaced. Did the sellers lie? Probably not. Should the buyers have done more discovery? Probably.

Licensees can provide a blank seller disclosure form to buyers early in the transaction. Then, buyers can decide in advance if there are issues about which they are concerned and which may not be included in the disclosure statement. It is important that buyers determine what is most important to them; not all of their concerns may be addressed in the owner disclosure document.

Licensees should encourage buyers to hire experts, such as a home inspector, to discover problems, if any, with the property.
